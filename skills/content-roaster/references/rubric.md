# Content Roaster rubric

Use the applicable dimensions only. Do not turn the rubric into a checklist dump.

## 1. Audience and problem

- Can the intended reader identify that the artifact is for them?
- Is the problem concrete enough to recognize in real work?
- Does the artifact over-assume awareness, maturity, budget, or urgency?

## 2. Positioning and category

- Is the category understandable before the reader must decode jargon?
- Is differentiation meaningful or just adjective substitution?
- Does the artifact explain why this approach exists versus obvious alternatives?

## 3. Promise and outcome

- Is the primary promise bounded, specific, and connected to reader value?
- Does the copy imply outcomes beyond the supplied proof?
- Is a large promise diluted by generic phrasing?

## 4. Mechanism

- Does the reader understand *how* the promised outcome becomes plausible?
- Is mechanism concrete enough to distinguish the offer?
- Is mechanism confused with feature inventory?

## 5. Claim-evidence fit

- Are quantitative/comparative/outcome claims sourced or queued for verification?
- Is proof relevant to the exact claim, audience, and scope?
- Does testimonial/social proof substitute for technical/economic proof it cannot support?
- Is the strongest proof buried after the high-cost CTA?

## 6. Proof debt

- Which claims carry evidence burden that is not yet paid?
- Is the debt external (needs research/data) or editorial (proof exists but is absent/misplaced)?
- Would closing the proof debt change the reader decision?

## 7. Objections and risk

- What material objections block the requested action?
- Are risk, switching cost, implementation effort, price, credibility, and fit handled at the right stage?
- Is the artifact defending against objections the reader probably does not have while ignoring the obvious one?

## 8. Commitment ladder and CTA

- Is requested commitment proportional to proof earned so far?
- Is there a lower-friction next step when the reader cannot responsibly commit yet?
- Does CTA text describe the actual next state rather than generic "Get started" language?

## 9. Information architecture

- Does each section answer the next reasonable reader question?
- Is the strongest evidence placed before the point where it is needed?
- Does the artifact repeat slogans instead of advancing the decision?

## 10. Specificity and abstraction

- Are nouns and mechanisms concrete?
- Do buzzwords replace falsifiable meaning?
- Could a competitor use the same paragraph unchanged?

## 11. Trust and qualification

- Are limitations, scope, prerequisites, and caveats where they materially affect the claim?
- Does confidence language exceed proof?
- Are guarantees, comparisons, or "best" claims appropriately bounded?

## 12. Editorial density

- Does each section add new decision-relevant information?
- Are examples doing work or decorating the page?
- Can sentences be simplified without deleting substance?

## 13. Voice and human readability

- Is tone consistent with buyer risk and brand context?
- Are there generic AI-shaped phrases, fake contrast structures, or mechanical repetition?
- Do not penalize concise structure merely because it resembles common AI output.

## 14. Technical documentation

When profile/lens is documentation, inspect prerequisites, environment/version assumptions, exact examples, error handling, rollback/recovery, and whether the documented success condition is testable.
